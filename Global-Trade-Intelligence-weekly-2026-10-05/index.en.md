## 1. Executive summary

Washington moved this week from tariffs to outright import bans. On September 29, Canadian motorcycles, whey, molasses, nonalcoholic beer, alcohol, and dairy stopped being dutiable goods and became prohibited ones. It is the first denial-of-entry measure of the US–Canada trade war, layered on top of 50% Section 338 duties in force since August (no USMCA exemption) and Canada's mirrored 15/25/50% counter-tariffs on 335 US items.

Deadlines are driving the agenda now. The Graham Act's October 18 deadline for secondary tariffs of up to 100% on the top buyers of Russian energy is thirteen days out, with China, India, and Turkey most exposed. Brussels takes up the Franco-German rapid-response trade weapon on October 15, days after Beijing opened an anti-dumping probe into EU p-nitrotoluene. Retaliation is outpacing negotiation this autumn.

Multilateralism stalled this week. G20 trade ministers in Milwaukee agreed on one of four agenda items. The US–China "30-for-30" tariff-relief lists, $30B a side, cut the trade-weighted US tariff on Chinese goods from 27.3% to 26.0%, per HSBC. The durable achievement is institutional rather than arithmetic: a standing Board of Trade, not the tariff math.

In enforcement, CBP issued two forced-labor Withhold Release Orders on Indonesian palm oil, and the DOJ's trade-fraud campaign added another case: a $5.15M False Claims settlement over Chinese LEDs declared as Taiwanese. Freight markets diverged. Transpacific container rates held firm into Golden Week while Asia–Europe fell for a twelfth consecutive week on returning Suez capacity. Dry bulk posted its worst week in over a month as Capesize earnings collapsed 12.8%.

### A1 — US import bans on Canadian goods (detail)

The September proclamations built a two-wave structure. Wave one, effective September 15, re-scoped the 50% Section 338 product list. Added at 50%: specialty cheeses, modified fats and oils, bovine hides and upholstery leather, raw and dressed furskins, recreational motorboats, specialty paper, select steel and aluminum items, metal fittings and welding inputs, golf carts, furniture and lamps, ATVs, and additional dairy. Removed: rock salt, sugar, cement, switchgear assemblies, toilet paper, fishing-rod parts, and bulk whiskies and liqueurs. Wave two, September 29, went further than any tariff: outright import bans, meaning denial of entry, on Canadian motorcycles (including BRP Can-Am Spyder and Canyon models), whey, molasses, nonalcoholic beer, alcoholic beverages, and dairy. Goods already on the water or in a bonded warehouse before September 29 stay subject to the 50% duty rather than the ban. ([tariffcalculator2026.com](https://tariffcalculator2026.com/canada-section-338-september-15-29-what-changes), [asrwe.com](https://asrwe.com/asr-university/us-canada-import-ban-alcohol-motorcycles-section-338-expansion-september-2026))

Importers most often misunderstand the stacking rule. The 50% Section 338 duty carries no USMCA exemption; a CUSMA certificate does nothing against it. But the separate 10% Section 301 forced-labor tariff on Canadian goods *is* avoided with a valid USMCA claim. So a covered non-USMCA good can face 50% plus 10%, for a combined 60%. ([tarifflens.ai](https://www.tarifflens.ai/blog/section-338-canada-retaliation-september-2026-importers-guide))

### A2 — Indonesian palm oil WROs (detail)

CBP's September 29 orders target palm oil and derivatives from Mitra Aneka Rezeki (MAR) and Hardaya Inti Plantation (HIP). Information reviewed for the orders included worker interviews, payroll slips, harvest quotas, photographs, NGO and government reports, news media, and academic research. MAR workers showed nine ILO forced-labor indicators, among them retention of identity documents and isolation. HIP showed seven: debt bondage, wage withholding, deception, excessive overtime, intimidation, abusive conditions, abuse of vulnerability. At all US ports, detention is immediate. Importers have three options: export the goods, destroy them, or prove clean sourcing. ([thompsonhine.com](https://www.thompsonhine.com/insights/cbp-issues-withhold-release-orders-on-two-indonesian-palm-oil-producers/), [ghy.com](https://www.ghy.com/trade-compliance/2026-withhold-release-orders-wros/))

### A3 — Graham Act (detail)

Signed September 18 with bipartisan support, H.R. 5334 puts the Russia sanctions regime on statutory footing and creates two mandatory tariff tracks, both due by October 18. Track one: up to 500% on all Russian-origin goods. Track two: up to 100% secondary tariffs on countries that ranked among the top five importers of Russian crude or gas and made new purchases within 30 days of enactment. Most exposed are China, India, and Turkey; Slovakia, Hungary, and the UAE face elevated risk. EU members are assessed individually. The escape valves are narrow: a natural-gas exception (imports below 15% of Russia's total gas exports, plus what the Act calls "significant steps" to reduce them) and national-interest waivers. All duties stack on existing Section 301 and 232 duties. ([lexblog.com](https://www.lexblog.com/2026/09/24/the-lindsey-graham-sanctions-act-what-businesses-need-to-know-about-the-new-tariffs/), [jdsupra.com](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/new-sanctions-and-tariff-power-under-4624517/), [tradepractitioner.com](https://www.tradepractitioner.com/2026/09/sanctions-by-statute-the-graham-act-and-tariffs-on-russias-energy-buyers/))

### A4 — Polysilicon Section 232 (detail)

The terms were set by an August 6 proclamation and a Commerce temporary final rule dated September 24. From December 4: minimum import prices of $21/kg for polysilicon, $100/kg for ingots and wafers, $0.22/W for cells, and $0.38/W for modules, plus a 15% ad valorem tariff on derivatives. Raw polysilicon itself is exempt from the 15%. The UK gets 10%; Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Switzerland, Liechtenstein, and the EU are capped at 15% total including MFN. Anti-surge and stockpiling restrictions are already in force for the September 22 to December 4 window. The market is front-running the deadline: US module offer prices already sit at the $0.38/W floor, up 40.7% since August (from $0.27/W), to the benefit of Hanwha Solutions and OCI. Industry estimates put the added equipment cost near $10M per 100 MW of project capacity. ([mondaq.com](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/export-controls-trade-investment-sanctions/1848548/polysilicon-and-derivatives-in-the-bullseye-for-major-new-tariffs-and-other-restrictions), [en.sedaily.com](https://en.sedaily.com/finance/2026/09/28/us-solar-module-prices-jump-40-percent-before-import-curbs), [logisticusgroup.com](https://logisticusgroup.com/new-solar-tariffs-land-december-4-heres-how-to-keep-your-project-on-budget-and-on-schedule/))

### A5 — EU rapid-response tool (detail)

On October 5, Macron and Merz wrote to von der Leyen proposing a "systemic reaction" instrument: country-agnostic, triggerable by a minority of member states (which would break the qualified-majority norm), authorizing "decisive" measures up to immediate cutoff from the internal market. Paris and Berlin frame it as the EU's answer to US Section 301 and China's critical-mineral export controls. One German official called it a "second-strike weapon." A parallel diversification instrument would cap single-country dependence for critical imports. The backdrop: the EU goods deficit with China hit €359.9B in 2025 and €103B in Q2 2026, the highest since Q3 2022. The Commission's enforcement chief cites import surges hitting machinery, textiles, metals, and chemicals, not just EVs. ([reuters.com](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/france-germany-propose-new-rapid-eu-trade-response-tool-2026-10-05/), [wsj.com](https://www.wsj.com/world/europe/germany-and-france-are-proposing-a-new-weapon-to-counter-flood-of-chinese-goods-f723dfdf), [brusselsmorning.com](https://brusselsmorning.com/eu-china-trade-tensions/104764/))

### A6 — China retaliates first (detail)

On October 3, MOFCOM (Announcement No. 44) opened an anti-dumping investigation into p-nitrotoluene from the EU. The timing, days before Beijing trade talks and squarely against the Franco-German proposal, functions as a warning. MOFCOM separately warned of "firm" retaliation against any EU rapid-response instrument. ([vespernews.com](https://www.vespernews.com/en/news/7dd758b7-b501-4c41-b2ee-13cb57ed832f))

## 3. Top stories — deep dives

### 3.1 G20 trade ministers fracture in Milwaukee (Sept 30 – Oct 2)

Chaired by USTR Jamieson Greer under the US G20 presidency, trade ministers met in Milwaukee with four agenda items. They agreed on one: a joint statement condemning the weaponization of food trade through coercive actions. The other three produced nothing.

**The scorecard.** On excess capacity, a draft backed by most members was blocked by a China-led minority resisting subsidy language; Greer said the presidency was "severely disappointed." For context on Washington's posture: the US has opened excess-capacity investigations into what Greer called countries "from Norway to Bangladesh," citing autos, batteries, paper, and semiconductors alongside Chinese steel. On forced labor, no joint text emerged, so the US, Mexico and Argentina issued a separate declaration. On MFN reform, the US opened a debate on whether unconditional most-favoured-nation treatment "fails to promote reciprocity" and "creates free riders," but sought no joint statement. ([barchart.com](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/4917577/g20-trade-talks-yield-little-progress-while-tensions-simmer) (AP), [reuters.com](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/g20-trade-chiefs-denounce-food-trade-coercion-not-excess-factory-capacity-2026-10-01/), [nationpress.com](https://www.nationpress.com/all/g20-milwaukee-talks-eye-more-diesel-globally))

**The sidelines told a different story.** India amended its Foreign Trade Policy in July 2026 to ban imports of goods made wholly or partly with forced labour, and Goyal raised it in Milwaukee while running a two-track strategy: defend positions in plenary, push bilaterals on the margins. US-brokered discussions are expected to put more diesel into the global market, though parties, volumes and timetable are undisclosed. And there was no breakthrough on the US–Canada dispute; Trump banned roughly $1B of Canadian imports (alcohol, dairy, motorcycles) during the week. ([beatsinbrief.com](https://beatsinbrief.com/2026/10/03/milwaukee-g20-trade-ministers-piyush-goyal-india-positions/), [businessmirror.com.ph](https://businessmirror.com.ph/2026/10/02/g20-trade-talks-yield-little-progress-while-tensions-simmer/))

The failure to produce an outcome document is itself the signal. Multilateral trade governance can no longer constrain the current tariff wave; the action has moved permanently to unilateral and bilateral tracks. Watch the G20 leaders' summit in Miami in December for whether Milwaukee's fractures surface at head-of-state level. ([nationpress.com](https://www.nationpress.com/international/g20-trade-talks-end-without-deal-goyal))

### 3.2 US–China "30-for-30": lists published, economics marginal

The September 23–25 Trump–Xi summit in Washington produced the new US–China Board of Trade's "30-for-30" framework, and on September 28 both governments published reciprocal tariff-relief product lists: each side may de-tariff roughly $30B of the other's goods. ([cnn.com](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/28/business/us-china-tariff-cuts-60-billion-intl?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta), [kpmg.com](https://kpmg.com/us/en/taxnewsflash/news/2026/09/us-china-trade-framework-tariff-reductions.html))

**Two lists, two philosophies.** The US list runs 77 HTS lines, all Chinese consumer goods: toys, fireworks, tableware, blankets, microwaves, Christmas-tree lamps, highchairs, child car seats, and electric shavers. Wi-Fi and Bluetooth-connected toys are carved out on data-security grounds, and all apparel is excluded. China's list runs 1,619 lines: corn, wheat, sorghum, meat, dairy, oils, fish, seafood, timber, plus cosmetics and medical devices. Whole soybeans, the largest US farm export to China, are notably absent. The US list covers about 7% of US imports from China (2024 values); China's covers about 18% of its imports from the US.

**The math vs. the institution.** HSBC (September 29): the trade-weighted US tariff on Chinese goods falls only from 27.3% to 26.0%. Over 90% of listed goods revert to MFN ("normal") duties, and adjustments under the terms of reference occur at most once a year. On the side deals: China will import at least 10M metric tons of US coal in 2027 and again in 2028, an agriculture market-access working group launched, and rare-earth shipment levels are to be "returned to appropriate levels" (China met only about two-thirds of prior supply commitments; the US–China goods deficit is down 40% since January 2025, to $140B). No tariff cuts are in force yet. USTR must still announce rates and dates. ([exencialrp.substack.com](https://exencialrp.substack.com/p/hsbc-sees-us-china-30-for-30-deal) (HSBC via Substack), [lexblog.com](https://www.lexblog.com/2026/09/29/u-s-china-board-of-trade-30-for-30-identifies-products-recommended-for-reduced-tariffs/), [tradersunion.com](https://tradersunion.com/news/financial-news/show/3528484-us-china-lower-tariff-framework/))

Setting the ceremony aside, the framework's significance is institutional: a standing Board of Trade with working procedures, not the tariff arithmetic. For sourcing planners: apparel's exclusion preserves the China-to-Vietnam/Bangladesh/India diversification incentive, while home textiles (blankets, bed and table linen, and curtains) could face stiffer Chinese competition if relief lands. ([textalks.com](https://textalks.com/us-china-tariff-excludes-apparel-but-opens-door-to-chinese-home-textiles/))

### 3.3 C.H. Robinson to acquire RXO — $5.8B

On October 5, C.H. Robinson (Nasdaq: CHRW) agreed to acquire RXO (NYSE: RXO) in a cash-and-stock deal worth $5.8B. The terms: $17.25 in cash plus 0.0856 CHRW shares per RXO share, or $30.25 a share, a 29% premium to the October 2 close. Both boards approved unanimously. MFN Partners (17% of RXO) and Orbis, the largest holder, back the deal. Close is expected in H1 2027, pending regulatory approval and an RXO shareholder vote. ([rxo.com](https://rxo.com/news/c-h-robinson-to-acquire-rxo-redefining-the-future-of-third-party-logistics-while-unlocking-significant-shareholder-value/), [reuters.com](https://www.reuters.com/business/ch-robinson-buy-freight-broker-rxo-58-billion-2026-10-05/))

**Reading the market reaction.** RXO shareholders will own about 11% of the combined company, which would carry a combined enterprise value above $25B; RXO folds into CHRW's North American Surface Transportation division (over two-thirds of revenue). Management promises $300M in net run-rate cost synergies within two years via CHRW's "Lean AI" operating model: shared services, vendor consolidation, real estate, insurance. The market reaction followed the usual pattern. RXO jumped about 20% (to $28.10–28.49); CHRW fell 12% premarket ($150 vs. $157.72 close), the discount acquirers routinely absorb. Morgan Stanley advised CHRW, Goldman Sachs advised RXO. One detail worth flagging: RXO rose 16.2% in the two sessions before the announcement on 3.67M-share volume, against a norm of about 1.9M, a visible pre-announcement run. ([freightwaves.com](https://www.freightwaves.com/news/breaking-c-h-robinson-acquiring-rxo), [financial-news.co.uk](https://www.financial-news.co.uk/c-h-robinson-to-buy-rxo-in-5-8bn-cash-and-stock-deal/), [inboundlogistics.com](https://www.inboundlogistics.com/articles/c-h-robinson-to-acquire-rxo-in-5-8-billion-deal/))

Freight-brokerage consolidation tends to accelerate in soft-rate periods, when larger operators buy density and technology instead of building it. The combined entity would hold real pricing power in North American truckload, which points to firmer contract negotiations for shippers and tougher conditions for smaller brokers. The "Lean AI" synergy plan is also a signal about where brokerage labor costs go next: into automation. (Newsquawk via [financial-news.co.uk](https://www.financial-news.co.uk/c-h-robinson-to-buy-rxo-in-5-8bn-cash-and-stock-deal/))

### 3.4 India–US: "short strokes" but no deal

On the G20 sidelines (October 1–2), USTR Greer met Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal and declared the talks in the "short strokes," then added: "I don't think there's something imminent." A Trump–Modi call preceded the meeting; another leaders' call is expected "very soon." ([thehindubusinessline.com](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/news/nothing-imminent-us-trade-representative-greer-on-india-trade-deal-modi-trump-to-talk-soon/article71536059.ece/amp/), [nationpress.com](https://www.nationpress.com/international/india-us-trade-deal-in-short-strokes-greer))

**The pressure points.** Indian goods face an extra 10% Section 301 forced-labor tariff since July 24, one layer of a 60-economy action, and a second 301 probe on structural excess capacity (16 economies including India) is pending and could hit steel. The sticking points are familiar: agriculture access, digital trade, and India's Russian-oil purchases, the last now sharpened by the Graham Act's up-to-100% secondary tariffs. Paradoxically, despite the duties, India's merchandise exports to the US rose 21.83% year on year in August. The rupee sits near 96.3 to the dollar. Both sides still cite the $500B bilateral trade target for 2030. Finance Minister Sitharaman (October 4) said talks have "reached a plateau," with further concessions hard to secure. ([thefinancialeconomy.com](https://www.thefinancialeconomy.com/india-us-trade-talks/), [jagranjosh.com](https://www.jagranjosh.com/general-knowledge/indiaus-trade-deal-not-imminent-why-is-it-being-delayed-who-has-the-advantage-what-are-indias-options-1820012731-1), [english.dainikjagranmpcg.com](https://english.dainikjagranmpcg.com/top-stories/india-us-trade-talks-reach-plateau-nirmala-sitharaman/article-36152))

India is the swing variable in two stories at once: the China+1 sourcing map and the Graham Act's secondary-tariff targets. A deal would open one of the few tariff-relief valves available to US importers this year. Failure keeps Indian exporters, and their US buyers, in limbo into peak season.

### 3.5 Drones: tariffs up, export controls down

Two opposite-moving US drone actions, both rooted in an August 13 double-header. First, a Section 232 proclamation (Proclamation 11055) imposing 100% tariffs on drones over 25kg or with thermal imaging (plus docking stations and critical components, Annex I) and 25% on smaller non-thermal drones (Annex II), effective September 3; components (Annex III) face 25% from February 9, 2027. Allied rates apply: UK 10%, Japan/Korea/Taiwan/Switzerland/Liechtenstein/EU capped at 15% total. Second, a BIS final rule easing export controls: sub-3-hour-endurance UAVs drop to AT-only control under ECCN 9A012, with the STA license exception available for Country Group A:5. Military end-users in China, Russia, Belarus, and Venezuela stay restricted. ([whitehouse.gov](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/08/adjusting-imports-of-unmanned-aircraft-systems-and-unmanned-aircraft-systems-components-into-the-united-states/), [jdsupra.com](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/two-us-drone-actions-reshape-export-8075367/) (Wiley), [tbgfs.com](https://tbgfs.com/csms-69738151-guidance-section-232-duties-on-imports-of-unmanned-aircraft-systems-and-unmanned-aircraft-systems-components/))

Read together, the two moves amount to a dual-track industrial policy: restricting drone imports while easing drone exports. Commercial buyers now face a divided market, with allied-supply-chain products at 10–15% duties and Chinese-origin products at 25–100%.

### 3.6 US goods deficit hits $132.6B in August

The advance August goods deficit widened 11.5% to $132.6B. Imports reached $336.1B (up 5.5%) on industrial supplies and AI-buildout capital goods; exports were $203.4B (up 1.9%). Trade has now been a drag on GDP for the third consecutive quarter, a complication for an administration arguing that tariffs shrink the deficit. ([reuters.com](https://www.reuters.com/business/us-goods-trade-deficit-widens-sharply-august-2026-09-30/))

### 3.7 De minimis remains closed; IEEPA refunds proceed

**De minimis stays shut.** The Court of International Trade (August 13, *Axle of Dearborn* / Detroit Axle case) upheld the IEEPA-based rescission of the $800 de minimis exemption, ruling that withdrawing a "privilege" does not constitute imposing a tariff, so it survives the Supreme Court's February ruling that struck down IEEPA tariffs. The global de minimis exemption ended August 29, 2025; Congress's own closure takes effect in July 2027. ([reuters.com](https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/us-court-backs-trumps-power-close-de-minimis-tariff-exemption-2026-08-13/), [openclassactions.com](https://openclassactions.com/tariff-class-actions.php))

**The refund machine grinds on.** CBP's CAPE portal, the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries system inside ACE, live since April 20, is processing the roughly $165B in IEEPA duties collected, with interest accruing near $650M a month. CAPE Phase 3 deploys October 6 to handle refunds on reliquidated entries. ([thompsonhine.com](https://www.thompsonhine.com/insights/cbp-confirms-october-6-2026-launch-of-phase-3-of-the-ieepa-tariff-refund-process/))
